LARK

Low-cost, occlusion-resilient multi-camera optical tracking for image-guided surgery

Commercial optical trackers for image-guided surgery cost US$8,000 to US$25,000 and lose the instrument the moment a hand or a drape blocks the line of sight. LARK is an open-source alternative that puts a ring of RGB cameras on the surgical light itself, so that some camera almost always sees the tool, and fuses their estimates with an adaptive Kalman filter.

Live trajectory tracking on the head phantom: the five camera views and the live visualization as the pointer follows grooves on the cranial surface. Full supplementary video.

What it does

  • Tracks a surgical pointer and a static patient reference with ArUco fiducials seen from multiple calibrated cameras, using two complete methods: multi-view monocular pose fusion and multi-view triangulation.
  • Reaches median target registration errors of 0.64 mm (static) and 0.73 mm (dynamic).
  • Degrades gracefully as cameras are occluded or removed, instead of dropping out.
  • Tracking hardware costs under US$1,000, an order of magnitude below commercial systems.
Left: camera ring, pointer, static reference and head phantom. Right: the ring fixture that mounts on the operating-room light.

My role

I led the four-student team, co-designed and fabricated the light-mounted camera fixture, co-implemented multi-camera calibration and fiducial tracking, and developed the adaptive Kalman-filter fusion with online per-camera noise estimation. The project won 2nd place in the McGill Engine Capstone Design Prize for Entrepreneurship and led to OPSIS (2024–2026), a co-founded venture that explored commercializing the tracker through McGill Engine’s TechAccel program and received the Ian McLachlin Prize for Entrepreneurship. In January 2027, LARK will be featured in Coup de génie, a Télé-Québec science series hosted by NASA JPL engineer Farah Alibay (episode “Navigateur chirurgical”, airing January 25).

The preprint is on arXiv and the manuscript is submitted to Medical Image Analysis (Sideris et al., 2026). Code, calibration tools, marker generation and Raspberry Pi camera nodes are on GitHub; hardware designs and software are also published on the NIST lab software page, with datasets on the data page.

    LARK: A Low-Cost, Accurate, Occlusion-Resilient, Kalman Filter-Assisted Tracking System for Image-Guided Surgery
    George Sideris, Justin Cree, Andrew Stirling, and 3 more authors
    arXiv preprint, Oct 2026
    Submitted to Medical Image Analysis. An earlier version appeared on Research Square (doi: 10.21203/rs.3.rs-7622287/v1)
    Abs arXiv Bib HTML Video Code

    Image-guided surgery (IGS) depends on accurate tracking of surgical instruments to provide real-time navigation relative to anatomical structures. Commercial stereo infrared trackers are accurate but prone to occlusion and cost-prohibitive for many settings. This work presents LARK, a multi-camera optical tracking system using commodity RGB hardware and multi-view redundancy and fusion. We develop and evaluate two complete tracking methods: multi-view monocular pose fusion and multi-view triangulation. Both methods are assessed under varying occlusion levels using a precision-machined grid and an anatomical head phantom, and compared against a gold-standard stereo infrared system. With five cameras and adaptive Kalman filtering, LARK achieves median target registration errors of 0.64 mm for point localization with triangulation and 0.73 mm for trajectory tracking with pose fusion on the machined grid. Camera-subset experiments show graceful degradation in adaptive pose-fusion accuracy as fewer views remain available. With tracking hardware costing under $1,000 USD, LARK provides a low-cost platform for image-guided surgery research. Hardware designs and software are publicly available at https://nist.mni.mcgill.ca/software/, and datasets at https://nist.mni.mcgill.ca/data/.
